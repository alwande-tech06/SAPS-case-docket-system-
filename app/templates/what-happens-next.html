South African Police Service

What happens after you report a crime

Emergency in progress? Call 10111. Do not use this form.

This page explains what the police must do once you report, what you will be asked to do, and what to do if nothing happens. Reporting online starts the process; your signed statement is still taken at the police station.

The process, step by step

  1. You submit the report Your report is routed automatically to the station responsible for the area where the incident happened. You do not need to work out which station that is.
  2. A police official reviews it The official checks your statement against the crime definitions to confirm that what you describe is a crime. The official may contact you for missing detail.
  3. A case docket is opened If a crime is disclosed, a docket is opened and registered, and you receive a case number by SMS. The police may not refuse to open a case because the suspect is unknown, because you did not bring the suspect to the station, or because the incident happened in another station's area.
  4. The docket goes to a detective The docket is handed to the detective service, classified by seriousness, and allocated to an investigating officer. You are told who is investigating.
  5. The investigation runs Statements are taken, evidence and exhibits are collected, and forensic material is submitted. You may be asked for more information or to identify a suspect.
  6. The docket goes to the prosecutor or is filed If there is a case to answer, the docket goes to the National Prosecuting Authority, which decides whether to prosecute. If the case cannot be taken further yet, it is filed under a recorded reason and reviewed again later.
  7. You are told the outcome You are notified of the outcome and the reason for it, and you may ask for reasons for a decision not to prosecute.

What you are expected to do

  • Go to the station to sign your statement. Bring your ID. An online report starts the process, but the affidavit must be signed.
  • Keep your case number. It is sent to you by SMS and you will need it for every enquiry.
  • Preserve evidence. Do not clean or repair the scene, keep damaged items, keep messages, receipts and photographs, and upload what you have.
  • Stay contactable. Answer the investigating officer, attend identification parades and attend court when asked.
  • Report changes. Tell the investigating officer about new information, recovered property, or a change of address or phone number.
  • Understand withdrawal. You may ask to withdraw a charge, but once the docket is with the prosecutor, the decision to proceed belongs to the National Prosecuting Authority, not to you or to the police.

What happens with the crime you are reporting

Open the type of incident you are reporting.

Theft

What the police do

  • Open a docket and register the stolen items as circulated, so they can be traced if recovered.
  • Check for witnesses, CCTV and any identification of a suspect.

What you should provide

  • A description of what was taken and its value.
  • Serial numbers or IMEI numbers, and proof of ownership such as an invoice or box.
  • Whether the item is insured, since your insurer will ask for the case number.

What to expect

  • If no suspect can be identified after all leads are followed, the docket is filed and re-examined periodically. It reopens automatically if the property is recovered.
Common assault

What the police do

  • Open a docket, record your injuries and take statements from any witnesses.
  • Trace and question the suspect where the suspect is identifiable.

What you should provide

  • How the assault happened and what injuries you suffered.
  • Photographs of visible injuries.
  • A medical report (J88) if you were treated. Ask the police for the J88 form to take to the doctor.
  • Your relationship to the person, if you know them.
See a doctor even if the injuries seem minor. A medical record is often the strongest evidence in an assault case.
Sexual offence

Go to a police station, hospital or Thuthuzela Care Centre now

Do not wait, and do not give detail on this form. Your statement is taken in private by a specially trained officer, and you may ask for an officer of a particular gender and to be interviewed in your own language.

Before you go, if you can

  • Do not bathe, wash, change clothes or clean the scene.
  • Keep the clothing you were wearing, in a paper bag if possible.

What the police do

  • Open a docket. A sexual offence report can never be turned away.
  • Arrange a medical examination and collection of forensic evidence.
  • Refer the docket to the specialised unit that investigates these offences.
  • Offer you counselling and victim support services.
Forensic evidence can be collected best within 72 hours, but you can still report and be helped after that.
Domestic violence

What the police must do

  • Open a docket where a criminal offence is disclosed, such as assault, threats or damage to property.
  • Inform you of your right to apply for a protection order, and how to do it.
  • Help you find a safe place and medical treatment if you need them.
  • Serve and enforce an existing protection order, including arresting for a breach of it.

What you should provide

  • Your relationship to the person and whether you live together.
  • Whether a protection order exists, and its number.
  • Whether children were present or are at risk.
  • Whether you are in immediate danger.
A protection order is applied for at the magistrate's court, and it is separate from the criminal case. You can do both.
Fraud

What the police do

  • Open a docket and obtain records from the bank or service provider involved.
  • Refer complex or large commercial matters to a specialised unit.

What you should provide

  • What you were told or promised, and by whom.
  • The amount lost and how payment was made, with bank references.
  • Contracts, invoices, emails, WhatsApp messages and screenshots.

What to expect

  • Fraud investigations are slow because bank and phone records must be obtained through formal requests. Ask your investigating officer for updates rather than assuming nothing is happening.
Vehicle theft or hijacking

What the police do

  • Circulate the vehicle immediately so that it is flagged nationally.
  • Open a docket, and treat a hijacking as a serious, high-priority crime.

What you should provide

  • Registration number, VIN, make, model and colour.
  • Where it was parked or taken, and who had the keys.
  • Your tracking company details, if you have one.

What to expect

  • If the vehicle is recovered, the docket reopens automatically and you are contacted about collecting it after it is examined.
Burglary (housebreaking)

What the police do

  • Open a docket and, where appropriate, send a crime-scene team for fingerprints.
  • Circulate the stolen items.

What you should provide

  • The point of entry, and what was taken with values and serial numbers.
  • CCTV or alarm-company footage and reports.
  • Whether the premises are insured.
Do not tidy up or touch the point of entry until the police have examined the scene. Fingerprints are easily destroyed.
Malicious damage to property

What the police do

  • Open a docket and record the damage and its cost.
  • Trace and question the suspect where one is identified.

What you should provide

  • Photographs of the damage before repairs.
  • A quotation or estimate for repairs.
  • Proof that the property is yours.

What to expect

  • If someone is convicted, the court may order restitution, meaning the accused repairs or replaces what was damaged.
Robbery

What the police do

  • Treat it as a serious, high-priority crime, because force or the threat of force was used.
  • Open a docket, circulate what was taken, and check for CCTV and witnesses.
  • Record your injuries and arrange for a medical report where you were hurt.

What you should provide

  • What was taken, with serial or IMEI numbers.
  • What was said or done to make you hand it over, and whether a weapon was used.
  • A description of the people involved, and any injuries you suffered.
Robbery is not the same crime as theft. The force or the threat is what makes it robbery, so say exactly what was said or done.
Crimen injuria or intimidation

What the police do

  • Open a docket where your dignity was seriously impaired, or where you were threatened.
  • Obtain the messages, recordings or call records, and trace the sender where possible.

What you should provide

  • Exactly what was said or done, in the words that were used.
  • Screenshots showing the sender, the date and the time.
  • Whether it has happened before, and whether you have asked the person to stop.
Do not delete the messages, even if they are upsetting. They are the evidence, and a screenshot that does not show who sent it is much weaker.

The three possible outcomes of your report

OutcomeWhat it means
A docket is opened A crime is disclosed. You get a case number and an investigating officer.
Registered and transferred The incident happened in another station's area or belongs to a specialised unit. The case is still registered here first and then transferred. You are told where it went. You are never sent to another station yourself.
No docket opened Only for three reasons: the facts do not describe a crime (for example a purely civil debt dispute), the incident is already reported under an existing case number, or you decided not to lay a charge. The reason is recorded in writing, signed off by a commander, and sent to you with your escalation options.
The police may not decline to open a case because they think there is not enough evidence. Whether there is enough evidence is decided after an investigation, and by the prosecutor, not at the counter.

If your case is filed

A filed case is not a forgotten case. It is filed under a recorded reason and it reopens when circumstances change.

Reason it was filedWhat happens next
A suspect is wanted and a warrant is outReopens as soon as the person is arrested.
Property is outstandingReopens as soon as the property is recovered.
The perpetrator cannot be identifiedRe-examined periodically to see whether anything more can be done.
Insufficient evidence after investigationReviewed again after twelve months.
Forensic evidence was submittedReopens automatically if a DNA or fingerprint match identifies a suspect.

If you obtain new evidence at any time, take it to the investigating officer and ask for the case to be reopened.

Your rights as a victim of crime

  • Fairness, dignity and privacy. To be attended to promptly and interviewed in private, in the language of your choice.
  • To offer information. To give information during the investigation and trial, including a victim impact statement.
  • To receive information. To be told the case number, the status of the case, whether the suspect was arrested, charged, granted bail, convicted or sentenced, and to ask for reasons for a decision not to prosecute.
  • Protection. To be protected from intimidation, harassment and abuse connected to the case.
  • Assistance. To be referred to counselling, medical care and victim support services.
  • Compensation and restitution. To ask the court to order the accused to compensate you, or to return or repair what was taken or damaged.

Service Charter for Victims of Crime in South Africa (2004).

If you are not helped

  1. Ask for the commander on duty If an official will not assist you, ask to speak to the shift commander, the detective branch commander or the station commander.
  2. Escalate in this system Use the escalate button on your case page. An escalation is never sent back to the official who made the decision you are complaining about.
  3. SAPS National Complaints Centre 0800 333 177 or 0860 264 487.
  4. IPID The Independent Police Investigative Directorate investigates serious misconduct by police officials.

An official who refuses to register a legitimate complaint is committing misconduct and may face disciplinary steps, and in serious cases a criminal charge of defeating the ends of justice.
